What I'm working on

Nutflix and NFX pre-release

Peer-to-peer video where the people who share a video get paid by the people watching it. Videos are announced on Nostr, streamed from other viewers' computers, and paid for piece by piece in Cashu ecash. The creator sets the price, and you see it before you press play.

There are two versions. Nutflix is the desktop app. Version 0.1.2 for Linux came out in November, and it's a demo for now, not something to trust with real money. NFX is the open spec plus a Rust version built on iroh. It had its first releases in November too, with free video only for now: payments aren't wired in yet. The desktop app plays NFX videos as well.

There's a web client now too, at web.nutflix.xyz: NFX videos play in a plain browser with nothing to install. The browser can fetch the pieces straight from other viewers and seeders over WebRTC, and it checks every one before it plays. It's a small test network for now, with a test video on it.

bipnip in development

A network on Nostr for people who run Lightning nodes. It's a fork of Mostro, the peer-to-peer Bitcoin exchange that runs over Nostr and Lightning, and it keeps everything Mostro does. What it adds is for node runners. With a channel lease, you pay another node to open a channel to you, of a size and for a time you both agree on. Your node holds the key to the payment until it has checked the channel itself, so nobody can take the money without opening it. A ring is a few node runners who each open a channel to the next. And a Nostr key and a Lightning node can vouch for each other with signatures anyone can check.

It's early. So far a lease completes between two real LND nodes on a test network, and the buyer gets the payment back if the channel never opens. Rings aren't built yet, and none of it is ready for real money.

vortex-ng in development

A coinjoin is one Bitcoin transaction that several people build together, so someone looking at the chain can't tell whose coins went where. JoinMarket has done this for years as an open market, with no coordinator in the middle. vortex-ng follows JoinMarket's protocol, carries the messages over Nostr relays, and is written in Rust. It picks up an idea from LnVortex, which is archived now: an output of the coinjoin can fund a new Lightning channel, so the channel opens straight out of the coinjoin.

It's early. On a test network, whole coinjoins already go through and confirm on Bitcoin Core. Opening channels from them isn't built yet, and nothing here should go near mainnet.

Private git over Nostr experimental

Git on Nostr already lets you host code without GitHub, but everything on it is public. I'm working on private repositories that can sit on a server you don't trust. cord-git encrypts everything before it's uploaded and handles the keys, members and invites, so the server only ever stores ciphertext. The server half is grasp-p, my fork of the ngit-grasp git server.

I publish my own open-source projects on Nostr too, with ngit, and mirror each one to GitHub. I also run git.sovtech.pro, my build of GitWorkshop, the web client for git over Nostr. I keep it close to upstream on purpose.

Smaller things

  • keytr: log in to Nostr with a passkey. Your key is encrypted with the passkey and kept on relays, so any device that has the passkey can sign you in. (source)
  • nbread: blogging on Nostr. Your posts are signed by your key, and nbread just shows them, quickly, at you.nbread.lol. (source)
  • Nostreward: a rewards system built on Nostr. You hand out redeem codes with a product, a promotion or anything else, and when someone redeems one the bot can zap them, repost their note, or let them into a private relay. (source)

Running my own stuff

I don't like depending on big platforms, so I host almost everything myself: git, email, passwords, notes, the VPN that ties my machines together, and the login for all of it. Each service runs in its own small virtual machine on hardware I own, and no third party decrypts the traffic on the way in. It's a lot of work. It's also the best way I know to learn how this stuff really works, and most of what I do for clients I tried on my own setup first.

I also run my own AI assistant, built on OpenClaw, the open-source agent. It does real work for me, not just chat. For anything private it uses Maple, private AI that runs its models inside secure hardware enclaves (TEEs), so not even Maple can read what I send it. I set up agents like this for clients too.
